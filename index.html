01 — The HaTS Feedback Program

Designing the end-to-end feedback infrastructure for an AI-native developer platform: continuous in-product survey, AI-powered open-text classification, real-time dashboard, and telemetry integration.

4Program components built
80%+LLM classification agreement
40K+Open-text responses analyzed

The Background

Firebase Studio launched in April 2025 as the natively agentic evolution of Project IDX. As the platform scaled to a much broader builder population, the existing feedback infrastructure (a single in-product satisfaction survey) hadn't yet caught up to either the diversity of users or the complexity of an agentic developer experience.


The Challenge

The first-generation HaTS survey provided a continuous pulse on satisfaction, but it couldn't answer the questions that actually drove product decisions: who was dissatisfied, why were they struggling, and which interactions preceded a negative experience? Manual analysis of open-ended responses didn't scale. Telemetry and survey data lived in separate silos. Stakeholders lacked timely access to synthesized feedback. As the user base evolved, the instrument itself needed to catch up.


My Role

Lead UX Researcher and program architect. I proposed treating HaTS not as a single survey but as layered feedback infrastructure, defined the four-component architecture, designed the V2 survey instrument, built the classification taxonomy, wrote and validated the LLM prompts, and drove the cross-functional partnership with engineering and Strategy Ops to build the dashboard and telemetry integration.

Program Architecture: Four Components

Rather than treating the HaTS as a single survey, I designed it as a layered feedback infrastructure where each component builds on the others.

1
The HaTS V2 Survey: from "what" to "who and why"
Redesigned the core in-product intercept survey to move beyond a generic satisfaction score. The key methodological change: replacing a generic "reason for use" question with mindset-specific questions mapped directly to the Builder Mindset Framework, enabling segmentation by Founder, Optimizer, Accelerator, and Learner intent. The V2 instrument was structured around builder intent rather than product satisfaction alone, covering primary goal, project context, ease-of-use against stated intent, AI usage, and an open-text improvement field. Rate-limited to once every 30 days per user to minimize fatigue.
2
LLM Analysis Automation: scaling qualitative insight
Designed the pipeline architecture and methodology, with a quant collaborator on the broader DevX research team building the automated implementation in Gemini 2.5 Pro (via Vertex AI API). The pipeline assigned each response a theme, sub-category, and tags, replacing hours of manual UXR work per cycle with a daily automated run. I wrote and iterated the classification prompts, defined the four-level feedback taxonomy through three rounds of inter-rater reliability testing with the quant collaborator (target: 80% agreement, consistently met or exceeded), and established the evaluation framework comparing LLM output against a manually-analyzed gold set.
3
The UX Metrics Dashboard: real-time access for all stakeholders
Drove the cross-functional partnership with Strategy Ops and Data Science to build a Looker Studio dashboard with daily data refresh, giving the full Firebase Studio team (PM, engineering, UXD, UXR) self-serve access to synthesized user feedback. The dashboard surfaced a Gemini-generated summary of current open-ended responses, ranked theme and sub-category breakdowns, filterable raw comments by theme and satisfaction level, and data export for offline analysis. Designed to give stakeholders the "what" quickly so UXR time went to explaining the "why."
4
HaTS + Telemetry Integration: connecting sentiment to behavior
Co-authored a formal intake proposal to unify HaTS survey responses with the platform's telemetry stream. The proposal asked engineering to embed survey metadata directly within telemetry events, enabling queries that linked in-product event sequences to the sentiment expressed by users who experienced them. At the time, diagnosing negative sentiment required manual, ad-hoc log analysis. The integration would have made it systematic.

What the System Surfaced

The first full cycle of LLM-classified feedback surfaced roughly 10 major themes spanning categories like agentic capability and quality, workflow integration, onboarding, platform performance, and production readiness. The signals aligned with gaps previously hypothesized for certain Builder Mindsets in adjacent research, validating that the feedback infrastructure was capturing the right level of analytical resolution to inform product strategy.

Firebase Studio launched April 9, 2025. Six weeks later, this report synthesized 49,678 survey responses from its first two months in market — delivering the platform's first rigorous, data-driven portrait of who was actually showing up, what they were trying to do, and where the experience was breaking down.

The Challenge

Firebase Studio launched into an uncertain market — a new agentic development environment positioned at the intersection of AI tooling and professional developer workflows. Within days of launch, the HaTS (Happiness Tracking Survey) was running, capturing real-time satisfaction signals via an in-product intercept displayed to users every 30 days.


But a continuous pulse on satisfaction isn't the same as understanding it. After two months of data collection, the team needed more than a CSAT number trending toward a target. They needed to know who was using the product, what they were trying to accomplish, where the experience was failing them, and which issues required immediate action versus strategic roadmap investment.


My Role

Lead author and analyst for the inaugural assessment report. I designed the analysis plan, synthesized quantitative survey data across CSAT, ease of use, reason for visit, AI usage, and app type dimensions, led the thematic analysis of thousands of open-ended responses, and authored the strategic recommendations presented to Firebase Studio product and engineering leadership.

Who Was Actually Using Firebase Studio

Before the assessment, assumptions about the user base were largely theoretical. The data told a more nuanced story across three distinct cohorts:

1
The creation-oriented majority — "Get started on something new" (50–51%)
Confirmed Studio's marketing resonance as a rapid ideation and prototyping tool. These users were satisfied (67% "somewhat easy" to "very easy") and drove strong top-of-funnel metrics — but their pain sharpened as projects grew more complex.
2
The growing retention cohort — "Continue working on an app" (20% → 27% MoM)
A 7-point jump from April to May was the most significant behavioral signal in the data. These returning users had moved past the initial prototype phase and were now grappling with performance, AI reliability, and publishing complexity. Their friction was real and consequential — they were the users most likely to churn.
3
The evaluator segment — "Learn more about Firebase Studio" (27% → 19% MoM)
A decline in evaluators suggested the initial wave of curiosity had peaked. This cohort — students, developers testing the waters — was most sensitive to onboarding clarity and the value proposition. Their satisfaction tracked closely with the quality of first-run experience.

Web dominated (54–55%) but mobile ambition was growing (32–35% and rising), with strong requests for Flutter and KMP framework support signaling where the next user segment was coming from.

Key Findings: Three Patterns Breaking User Trust

Critical performance and stability issues
The most pervasive complaint across all user segments: slowness, crashes, unresponsive UI, and workspaces perpetually locked in loading states. These were not minor inconveniences — they were fundamental blockers that invalidated the core promise of accelerated development. Users described waiting 10+ minutes on load screens and being unable to start applications at all. No feature improvement would retain users if the platform itself was unreliable.
AI reliability created a "prototype trap"
The Prototyper agent was praised for initial generation — and blamed for everything that followed. A confidence-competence gap emerged: the AI acted with high certainty while misunderstanding user intent, generating buggy code, getting stuck in debugging loops, and unable to fix the errors it created. This imposed a "babysitting tax" on users — instead of accelerating their work, they were constantly verifying, reverting, and supervising. A disjointed workflow between the Prototyper and Superchat further fragmented context, forcing users to re-establish intent at every transition.
A steep onboarding curve deterring new users
Studio was attracting a broad and non-traditional audience — including non-coders, students, and business professionals. But there was a near-complete absence of opinionated guidance for getting from initial idea to published app. New users encountered technical jargon without context, a confusing setup process, and anxiety around the billing model. The platform had no clear "guardrails to production" story for this audience.

Thematic Analysis: Five Pain Point Categories

Thematic analysis of thousands of open-ended responses produced a structured taxonomy — five dominant themes representing the architecture of user frustration in Firebase Studio's early months:

Platform Health & Performance

Speed & Latency · Bugs & Errors · Stability & Reliability. The most cited theme — a foundational trust issue.

AI & Agentic Capabilities

AI Intelligence & Reasoning · Code Generation Quality · Agentic Behavior & Autonomy · Multimodal & Advanced AI. A double-edged theme: source of both the product's greatest appeal and deepest frustration.

Developer Experience & Workflow

Integrations & Extensibility · Framework & Language Support · Deployment & Hosting · Environment & Configuration. Where returning users accumulated friction.

Onboarding & Usability

UI/UX Experience · Learning & Documentation · User Guidance & Onboarding. The entry barrier for new and non-technical users.

Production Readiness

Pricing & Billing Clarity · Free Tier & Quotas · General Value Proposition. Anxiety-generating for users considering longer-term commitment.

Strategic Recommendations & Impact

Priority 0 — Immediate
Stabilize the foundation
Diagnose and resolve workspace loading failures, UI lag, and platform crashes. Stabilizing the platform was the "stop the bleeding" imperative to prevent further user churn.
Priority 1 — Near-term
Improve AI reliability
Focus on context retention in the Prototyper, its ability to modify and debug code without introducing new errors, and overhauling the broken repository import and publishing workflow.
Priority 2 — Mid-term
Onboarding & expansion
Guided tutorials, project templates, and Flutter/Kotlin framework support — to drive growth among the non-technical audience Studio was already attracting.
Finding
Healthy but fragile
75% CSAT trending toward the 80% target (+2.98% MoM) — but the gap between initial excitement and practical utility was clear, and the growing retention cohort's pain was the leading indicator of what came next.

Survey Design: A Strategic Research Instrument

The survey was designed with conditional logic to capture meaningfully different experiences across four distinct usage states, allowing for targeted analysis of each cohort rather than a single averaged dataset.

1
Continue to use it: active retained users
Routed to questions about most valuable features, what could be improved, and open-ended feedback. The "what's working" signal.
2
Used it for a while, but stopped: lapsed users
Routed to structured churn reason selection and open-ended "what would bring you back" question. The richest signal for product priorities.
3
Poked around, never used seriously: light touch
Same churn battery as lapsed users. Captured friction in the earliest stages of the product experience.
4
Signed up but never used: dormant accounts
Routed to "why didn't you start" question, capturing barriers to first engagement, distinct from barriers to continued use.

The rationale for fielding approximately one week post-I/O: sufficient time for users to explore features and form real opinions, past the honeymoon effect, and early enough to identify churn drivers before they compounded.

Key Findings

AI features were both the strongest acquisition driver and the most-cited churn driver
The same features pulling users in were most reliably disappointing them, a structural product challenge rather than a customer-service issue.
Satisfaction was bimodally distributed
Among users who continued using the product, satisfaction was strong. The dissatisfied population was larger than the neutral population, suggesting active detractor risk rather than passive non-engagement.
Top-of-funnel conversion was the dominant churn point
A meaningful share of users came initially out of curiosity, and a substantial portion of that group churned before establishing real engagement. The gap between initial interest and value demonstration was the critical product challenge.
"Professional" described organizational context, not coding ability
The user base was a mix of technical and non-technical professionals. The combined professional population formed a majority, directly seeding the Builder Mindsets framework's foundational claim that user intent (not technical proficiency) was the most useful segmentation lens.

What Retained Users Valued

Retained users described two distinct strengths: the speed of going from idea to functional prototype, and (when AI features worked) a qualitatively different development experience compared to standalone tools. The delta between this satisfied experience and the frustration reported by churned users framed the core product challenge: the product's value claim was real, but inconsistently delivered.

04 — Builder Mindset Framework

The Background

Firebase Studio launched in April 2025 as the natively agentic evolution of Project IDX, anchored by the App Prototyping agent and Gemini in Firebase. Why Personas Weren't Enough

Personas describe

  • Who someone is
  • Static identity (role, skill level, demographics)
  • A fixed mapping per user
  • Best for: stable user populations

Builder Mindsets describe

  • What someone is trying to accomplish
  • Active intent (in this task, right now)
  • A fluid mapping that changes by task
  • Best for: rapidly expanding, AI-augmented populations

The same person can operate in different mindsets depending on the task. A senior engineer building a side project operates like a Founder; the same engineer refactoring a legacy codebase operates like an Optimizer. A model based on identity would miss the shift entirely.

"These aren't rigid personas; rather, they're fluid states that determine a user's approach, their definition of success, and the tools they require to build software safely and reliably."

— Understanding Builder Intent in the AI Era, DORA Research ↗

The Four Builder Mindsets

We identified four mindsets based on how users engage with AI-powered development tools. Builders often embrace multiple mindsets and shift between them depending on the task.

The Founder

Primary intent: Monetize

"How quickly can I get a working, shareable prototype to test with real users, investors, or early customers?"

AI as: The Dev Team, rapidly accelerating a vision and deferring traditional development

Success: Speed to MVP

Technical proficiency: Variable, often irrelevant

The Optimizer

Primary intent: Solve an Internal Problem

"Does the solution reliably and effectively solve the intended business problem? Does it save the company time or money?"

AI as: An Optimizer, evaluated for its ability to perform a specific, reliable task toward a defined outcome

Success: ROI

Technical proficiency: Technically literate, comfortable with APIs, data structures, and integration policies

The Accelerator

Primary intent: Increase Efficiency

"Does the generated code meet my professional standards? Does it make me demonstrably faster and more effective at my job?"

AI as: A Partner, augmenting existing skills and tools and enhancing workflows rather than replacing them

Success: Code quality and development velocity

Technical proficiency: Classically experienced; specific languages and frameworks are a primary asset

The Learner

Primary intent: Upskill

"Did I successfully build something? Did I gain confidence and learn something valuable in the process?"

AI as: A Tutor, providing foundational guidance and step-by-step instructions throughout the building process

Success: Personal growth and competence

Technical proficiency: Defined by task-level proficiency; an ephemeral state any builder can enter

Designing for Fluid Journeys

The framework's most operationally significant insight was about transitions. The same user moves across abstraction layers within a single session, from the highest conceptual layer of product vision down to the systems layer of integration and reliability, and into the infrastructure and code itself. The design implication is not to optimize for a mindset, but to master the transitions between them.

↑
Conceptual layer (Product vision)
High abstraction. User provides product vision; AI generates and visually refines the MVP. Example: "Create a restaurant deals app for my city where users pay an annual fee."
→
Systems layer (Integration and reliability)
Mid abstraction. User defines business rules or integration requirements; AI implements and verifies core logic. Example: "Connect to our Cloud bucket for attachments, set permissions to internal only."
↓
Infrastructure and code
Low abstraction. User encounters a specific problem; AI provides targeted, precise assistance. Example: "Refactor this component to use React hooks instead of class-based components."

Validation

The framework was independently validated three ways before adoption.

Qualitative validation
20 low-code journey mapping interviews with externally-recruited builders, conducted by a qualitative researcher on the broader DevX team. The mindsets held across the interview population.
Quantitative validation
Statistical testing of the Firebase Studio post-launch survey (n=2,285) using Qualtrics Stats iQ found that user intent was the most statistically significant predictor of CSAT. See Case 03 →
External validation
An industry analyst partner mapped 20 independently-recruited interviewees to the framework and adopted it as the structuring lens for their strategic recommendations. See Case 05 →

Three Engagement Objectives

I structured the Gartner engagement around three interlocking questions, each designed to move from diagnosis to strategy:

How pro devs choose
What are the top needs in the professional developer market, and what AI-enabled tools—both Google and competitors—do they use? What drives selection at the individual, team, and organizational level?

Key Finding 1: Perceived Fit Is Misaligned

↓
Users view Firebase Studio as a prototyping tool for Founders, not a professional development environment
Despite being built for the full SDLC, Firebase Studio's organic positioning as a rapid prototyping tool attracted Learners and Founders while signaling low relevance to Accelerators and Optimizers—the segments with the highest enterprise buying power.
↓
Teams have already adopted competing products for individual SDLC categories
Pro dev teams weren't waiting for a unified solution. They had assembled tool stacks: Cursor and GitHub Copilot for code generation, Replit for prototyping, Claude Code for complex tasks. Firebase Studio had to compete with an entrenched ecosystem, not a blank slate.
↓
Security, learning curve, and integration ranked as top selection criteria—not AI capability
Pro devs ranked security/compliance, ease of adoption, and integration with existing environments above raw AI functionality. Firebase Studio was being evaluated on dimensions it hadn't optimized for.

Key Finding 2: The Capability Gap

Gartner identified four capability categories not yet present in Firebase Studio that Accelerators and Optimizers considered essential—not nice-to-have:

Security, Governance & Quality
Built-in security and privacy checks, compliance guardrails, comprehensive testing, repository sanitization. The #1 gap for enterprise and regulated industries.
Brownfield / Existing Environment Operations
Integration with legacy codebases, cross-team sharing, extending and refactoring existing systems. "Even if I've connected Firebase Studio to my Git repo, it's not looking at that repo as it makes suggestions." — Associate CTO
Unique Business Relevance
Native understanding of organizational context: enterprise-specific codebase RAG, declarative infrastructure management, domain-aware suggestions.
Advanced Platform Functionality
Persistent model fine-tuning, multi-LLM support, architectural flexibility, and Agentic AI embedded within existing development environments.

The ASSESS → PRIORITIZE → IMPLEMENT Cycle

I designed the program's operating rhythm—a quarterly cycle that transformed raw CES data into actionable roadmap priorities. The cadence kept teams accountable and ensured leadership had a clear view of experience health.

1
Continuous Data Collection
6K monthly responses across 24 languages. Intercept surveys measuring ease (% rating 5-7) for four key digital interactions: buying, self-help, download, and managing access.
2
Behavioral Trace Analysis via PowerBI
For logged-in users who took the CES survey during a session, I instrumented cross-property session tracking — capturing page-level journey paths across all four digital properties, subscription and account context, and screen recordings of individual sessions. Surfaced in PowerBI: from top-line CES score down to individual user journeys, filterable by score, touchpoint, and account type. This let me identify exactly which journey paths were generating friction and driving score degradation — closing the loop between behavior and sentiment at the case level, not just the aggregate.
3
Actionable Drivers Initiative
Partnered with an Analyst and Data Scientist to run advanced data modeling—identifying the 3–5 most impactful drivers of effort score per workstream and presenting quarterly to stakeholder teams.
4

Monthly: comment topic trends, recent launch impacts, data deep-dives, and roadmap recommendations. Quarterly: leadership share-outs with full cross-workstream analysis.

Insights That Drove Decisions

Behavior closes the loop that scores open
Aggregate CES scores told us something was wrong. Cross-property session traces told us exactly where — and for whom. The ability to move from a 3-out-of-7 score to the screen recording of that user's broken journey path in under two clicks changed how quickly we could diagnose and prioritize friction.
Country-language gaps
My 2021 research share-outs surfacing different country and language experiences directly triggered the creation of a new country-language strategic initiative for Digital Platform & Experience.
Data can feel threatening
Declining CES scores created stakeholder defensiveness. I learned to pair metrics with behavioral context and always propose a path forward—not just document the problem.
Silo'd data doesn't work
The most valuable insights came from combining CES with behavioral analytics, support case data, and A/B test results — weaving a richer story of the customer experience.
The right segmentation changes the answer
Working with an analytics colleague, I modeled what our CES scores would have looked like had we separated student and professional users — two populations with fundamentally different effort tolerances and task contexts. The counterfactual showed that student users were systematically masking professional user friction in aggregate scores, with meaningful implications for roadmap prioritization.
Program structure is research
Building the operating model—the meeting cadence, report formats, workstream structure—was as important as the analysis. Without it, even great data goes unused.

Before

Vote FOR routine agenda items if sufficient information, otherwise AGAINST

IF sufficient_info = TRUE THEN vote = 'FOR'
ELSE vote = 'AGAINST'

Problem: Research team defines "sufficient." Client's policy says otherwise.

After

In Indonesia, vote MANUAL on routine agenda items

IF market = 'Indonesia'
AND item_type = 'routine'
THEN vote = 'MANUAL'

Result: Client reviews all routine proposals manually, per their actual policy.

Policy vs. Reality

My research mapped UNHCR's stated policy commitments against what the Somali community in Ard al-Liwa actually experienced:

UNHCR Policy (Stated)

Art. 43: Uphold education, vocational training, and self-reliance

Art. 79: Conduct surveys and opinion polls with urban refugees

Art. 141: Combat xenophobia through public campaigns

Reality on the Ground

Many SDO members were illiterate; UNHCR discontinued skills programs after 2 months citing budget

UNHCR had no direct contact with Ard al-Liwa; participatory assessments were rare

Refugees described feeling "in an open prison"—host country didn't want them, home too dangerous

Key Insights

Identity vs. Age: The "Elder" Problem
UNHCR selected community "elders" by age from case files—ignoring clan affiliation and experiential knowledge. This created deep distrust. SDO meetings with UNHCR were gridlocked: elders pushed for resettlement details; UNHCR focused on local integration. The mismatch was structural, not individual.
The Feedback Loop That Didn't Work
Refugees reached out to SDO → SDO met with UNHCR → no resolution → refugees protested → UNHCR convened another meeting → cycle repeated. No mechanism existed to break the loop because stakeholder priorities were fundamentally misaligned.

"Sometimes, it feels like being in an open prison; there's nowhere to go."

— Ma'lim Abdullahi, SDO Program Coordinator
