But the team's primary KPI wasn't the HaTS instrument ; it was CSAT, a single derived metric that compressed everything into one number trending toward a target of 80%. The tension was structural: HaTS is a measurement framework designed to capture attitudinal nuance; CSAT is a business metric designed to track progress against a goal. Using one to serve the other meant the instrument was simultaneously over-constrained (locked to a target) and under-specified (unable to explain why the number was moving). The survey was effective at flagging major pain points; but it couldn't answer the questions that actually drive product decisions: who is dissatisfied, why are they struggling, and which specific interactions preceded a negative experience? Manual analysis of open-ended responses was time-consuming and difficult to scale. Telemetry and survey data lived in separate silos. Stakeholders lacked timely access to synthesized feedback. And as the user base evolved, the survey instrument itself needed to catch up, reflecting the Builder Mindset framework rather than generic "reason for use" questions. KEY FINDINGS: THREE PATTERNS BREAKING USER TRUST Critical performance and stability issues The most pervasive complaint across all user segments: slowness, crashes, unresponsive UI, and workspaces perpetually locked in loading states. These were not minor inconveniences — they were fundamental blockers that invalidated the core promise of accelerated development. Users described waiting 10+ minutes on load screens and being unable to start applications at all. No feature improvement would retain users if the platform itself was unreliable. AI reliability created a "prototype trap" The Prototyper agent was praised for initial generation — and blamed for everything that followed. A confidence-competence gap emerged: the AI acted with high certainty while misunderstanding user intent, generating buggy code, getting stuck in debugging loops, and unable to fix the errors it created. This imposed a "babysitting tax" on users — instead of accelerating their work, they were constantly verifying, reverting, and supervising. A disjointed workflow between the Prototyper and Superchat further fragmented context, forcing users to re-establish intent at every transition. A steep onboarding curve deterring new users Studio was attracting a broad and non-traditional audience — including non-coders, students, and business professionals. But there was a near-complete absence of opinionated guidance for getting from initial idea to published app. New users encountered technical jargon without context, a confusing setup process, and anxiety around the billing model. The platform had no clear "guardrails to production" story for this audience. Builders often embrace multiple mindsets and shift between them depending on the task. The Founder PRIMARY INTENT: MONETIZE "How quickly can I get a working, shareable prototype to test with real users, investors, or early customers?" AI as: The Dev Team, rapidly accelerating a vision and deferring traditional development Success: Speed to MVP Context: Greenfield, unconstrained by existing org systems The Optimizer PRIMARY INTENT: SOLVE AN INTERNAL PROBLEM "Does the solution reliably and effectively solve the intended business problem? Does it save the company time or money?" AI as: An Optimizer, evaluated for its ability to perform a specific, reliable task toward a defined outcome Success: ROI Context: Constrained by security protocols, data integration requirements, team workflows The Accelerator PRIMARY INTENT: INCREASE EFFICIENCY "Does the generated code meet my professional standards? Does it make me demonstrably faster and more effective at my job?" AI as: A Partner, augmenting existing skills and tools and enhancing workflows rather than replacing them Success: Code quality and development velocity Context: Staff engineers, engineering managers, directors/VPs The Learner PRIMARY INTENT: UPSKILL "Did I successfully build something? Did I gain confidence and learn something valuable in the process?" AI as: A Tutor, providing foundational guidance and step-by-step instructions throughout the building process Success: Personal growth and competence Context: Junior employees, non-tech employees, students THE BUILDER SPACE: THREE ZONES The framework maps builder activity across two axes: System Readiness (how production-ready the environment needs to be) and Intent to Productionize (whether the goal is a real, deployable application). This surfaced three distinct zones that each mindset enters based on their task: The Professional Core Where users build, manage, and scale production-grade applications. High system readiness, high intent to productionize. The Accelerator and Optimizer operate primarily here. The Launchpad Where users bring ideas to life and prepare for market. High intent to productionize, lower initial system readiness. The Founder's primary territory, building toward real users. The Sandbox Where users explore what's possible and build new skills. Lower intent to productionize, lower system readiness. The Learner's home, and the entry point for every other mindset. DESIGNING FOR FLUID JOURNEYS The framework's most operationally significant insight was about transitions. The same user moves across abstraction layers within a single session, from high-level product vision (AI as the Experience) to systems integration (AI as a Systems Integrator) to precise code debugging (AI as a Teammate). The design implication is not to optimize for a mindset, but to master the transitions between them. ↑ AI as the Experience High technical abstraction. User provides product vision; AI generates and visually refines the MVP. Example: "Create a restaurant deals app for my city where users pay an annual fee." → AI as a Systems Integrator Mid-layer. User defines business rules or integration requirements; AI implements and verifies core logic. Example: "Connect to our Cloud bucket for attachments, set permissions to internal only." ↓ AI as a Teammate Low technical abstraction. User encounters a specific problem; AI provides targeted, precise assistance. Example: "Refactor this component to use React hooks instead of class-based components."
